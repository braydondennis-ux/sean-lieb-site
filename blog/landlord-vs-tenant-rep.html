Landlord vs. Tenant Rep: What Moves the Needle

By Sean Lieb · Jul 21, 2026 · 6 min read

People assume a retail deal is a tug-of-war where one side's broker wins and the other loses. In practice, the best outcomes happen when both sides are well-represented and well-prepared — the disagreements are just better informed.

So what does representation actually change? Not the rent number on the first email. It changes everything around it: the quality of the tenant, the certainty of the close, and the dozen lease terms that quietly determine whether a deal is good or merely done.

What a landlord rep is really doing

Good landlord representation starts long before a prospect calls. It's about knowing your center's true position in the market, curating the right merchandising mix, and protecting long-term value — not just filling a vacancy with whoever shows up first.

  • Pricing to the market, not the memory. Asking rents should reflect today's comparable deals and your center's real leverage.
  • Underwriting the tenant. A slightly lower rent from a strong operator often beats top dollar from a shaky one.
  • Protecting the rent roll. Co-tenancy, exclusives, use restrictions and options can help — or quietly hurt — the next five renewals.
The headline rent gets the attention. The lease terms determine the return.

What a tenant rep is really doing

On the tenant side, representation is about fit and leverage. The right space in the wrong trade area is still the wrong deal. A good tenant rep pressure-tests the location against the concept, then negotiates the terms that protect the business as it grows.

  • Site selection first. Demographics, traffic, co-tenancy and visibility before price.
  • Deal structure. Free rent, tenant improvement allowance, escalations and options often matter more than base rent.
  • Speed with discipline. The best spaces move quickly; being organized and credible wins them.

Where deals actually stall

In my experience the deals that fall apart rarely die over base rent. They die over the terms nobody negotiated carefully up front — the improvement allowance that didn't cover the buildout, the escalation that outran the tenant's sales, the exclusive that boxed the landlord out of a better tenant later. Representation is how those land mines get found before signatures, not after.

The takeaway

Whether you're an owner or an operator, the value of representation isn't squeezing the other side — it's walking in with better information and leaving with a deal that still looks smart in year three. That's the standard I hold every assignment to.
